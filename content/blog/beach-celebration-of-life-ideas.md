A beach celebration of life can feel open, quiet, and connected to a person who loved the water. It can also become difficult quickly if parking, wind, heat, accessibility, tides, and local rules are treated as afterthoughts.

Plan the practical pieces first. Then keep the ceremony simple.

## Choose the exact beach access

“Cocoa Beach” or “the Space Coast” is not specific enough for guests. Choose an access point and check:

- Parking capacity and current fees
- Restrooms
- Distance from parking to sand
- Stairs, ramps, boardwalks, and wheelchair access
- Shade or pavilion availability
- Group-size or special-event rules
- Restrictions on fires, glass, amplified sound, decorations, and pets

For a Brevard County location, begin with the county's [special-event permit information](https://www.brevardfl.gov/PlanningAndDevelopment) or the rules of the city managing that beach. Cocoa Beach publishes current [beach guidelines](https://www.cityofcocoabeach.com/192/Beach-Guidelines) and parking information.

Contact the managing agency directly before printing invitations. Rules and conditions change.

## Plan for the people who cannot walk on sand

Choose a boardwalk, pavilion, overlook, or accessible viewing area when older or disabled guests may struggle on soft sand. Provide chairs and tell guests exactly how far they will need to walk.

Someone can livestream or record the ceremony for relatives who cannot safely attend.

## Use the natural setting

You do not need elaborate decorations. Bring one framed photograph, a small arrangement secured against wind, and printed programs or memory cards weighted in a basket.

Avoid confetti, balloons, plastic flowers, glitter, and loose decorations that can become marine debris. Take everything back with you.

## Keep the program short

Sun, wind, surf noise, and standing on sand make long programs difficult. A simple order might be:

1. Welcome and safety note
2. Two-minute life story
3. Two or three planned memories
4. A song, reading, or quiet minute
5. One shared action
6. Invitation to continue gathering elsewhere

Use a portable microphone only if permitted and truly needed.

## Choose a shared action that leaves no litter

Guests can hold smooth stones and place them in a family bowl afterward, write memories on cards that go home with the family, take a silent shoreline walk, or each speak one word connected to the person.

If flowers are used, keep them contained unless the managing agency explicitly permits release. “Biodegradable” does not automatically mean appropriate for a public beach or wildlife.

## Understand ashes-at-sea rules

Scattering cremated remains from shore is not the same as burial at sea. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency states that cremated remains placed in ocean waters under the federal general permit must be at least three nautical miles from land, and the burial must be reported to EPA afterward. Review the current [EPA burial-at-sea requirements](https://www.epa.gov/marine-protection-permitting/burial-sea) and confirm state, local, and vessel-provider requirements.

A licensed funeral home or burial-at-sea provider can help the family handle this separately from the beach gathering.

## Prepare for Florida weather

Avoid the hottest part of the day. Check tides, wind, rain, lightning, and heat advisories. Name a backup location and decide in advance who will make the weather call.

Bring water, sunscreen, insect repellent, towels, weights or clips for paper, and a first-aid kit. Do not use a tent or large setup without permission.

## Preserve what guests share

Record the planned remarks when everyone agrees. Collect written memories and label every contributor. Photograph the display and gathering, but respect guests who do not want pictures during grief.

Afterward, those stories can become a small tribute booklet or a [custom memorial book](/memorial-tribute-books/) that lasts longer than the footprints in the sand.
