A family reunion gathers the exact people whose memories are hardest to collect the rest of the year. With a little preparation, the event can produce more than group photographs. It can create the raw material for a family history book.

The secret is to make contributing easy.

## Choose the book's purpose before the reunion

Decide whether the finished book will cover:

- The reunion itself
- One branch of the family
- A shared ancestor or couple
- A family place, farm, business, church, or migration
- Recipes and traditions
- Short memories from every household

A clear theme helps you ask better questions and prevents the project from becoming an unsorted archive.

## Create three collection stations

### 1. The photograph table

Invite relatives to bring copies or scans rather than irreplaceable originals. Provide cards for names, dates, locations, and ownership information.

Photograph the back of any image containing handwriting or notes.

### 2. The memory-card table

Use short prompts:

- The family story I heard most often was…
- A tradition I hope continues is…
- The funniest person in the family was… because…
- A place that shaped our family is…
- Something younger relatives should know is…

Ask contributors to print their full name and relationship to the family.

### 3. The recording corner

Choose a quiet room or shaded area away from speakers and crowd noise. Record five- to ten-minute conversations rather than attempting full interviews.

Post the questions so people can think before sitting down.

## Give interviewers defined assignments

Do not assume someone will “get interviews.” Assign names and time blocks. One person can speak with elders, another with cousins, and another with children.

Good reunion questions include:

- Who is the oldest relative you remember personally?
- What family story do you think may be exaggerated?
- Which relative were you most like?
- What did reunions look like when you were young?
- Which recipe or tradition belongs in the book?
- What family place should never be forgotten?

## Include children and teenagers

Ask younger relatives to draw the family home, interview an elder, photograph meaningful objects, or answer “What I want future cousins to know about our family.”

Teenagers can scan materials, monitor audio, or create a map of where relatives live now.

## Build a structure after collection

A reunion book might include:

1. A short family overview and tree
2. The reunion date and location
3. The shared ancestor or family place
4. Photographs and captions by generation
5. Short oral-history excerpts
6. Recipes and traditions
7. A “where we are now” map
8. Children and youth pages
9. A complete attendee list
10. Sources and acknowledgments

See our guide to [family history book formats](/blog/family-history-book-formats/) for more ways to organize the material.

## Protect privacy and originals

Tell people how the book will be shared. Do not publish addresses, private family conflicts, medical information, or stories about living people without permission.

Return original photographs promptly. Save scans and recordings in at least two places and keep a simple spreadsheet showing who contributed each item.

## Finish while the reunion is still fresh

Set a realistic deadline before the event. Send contributors a proof of their names and quotations. Print one complete proof copy before ordering for the whole family.
